❯ In the Two Lane Model, let $N_1(t),N_2(t)$ represent the number of cars in    
  lanes 1 and 2 of two parallel roads, with the congestion dynamics of the      
  congestion model, drivers change lanes from the busier toward the freer lane  
  at a rate proportional to the imbalance $N_1(t) - N_2(t)$.                    
                                                                                
  Assume $A_1,A_2,\lambda>0$, we have the following system of coupled ordinary  
  differential equations:                                                       
  \begin{align*}                                                                
  \frac{dN_1}{dt}                                                               
      &= A_1 - B(N_1) - \lambda (N_1-N_2), \\                                   
  \frac{dN_2}{dt}                                                               
      &= A_2 - B(N_2) + \lambda (N_1-N_2).                                      
  \end{align*}                                                                  
  In order to solve this system for $N_1(t)$ and $N_2(t)$, some re-arranging    
  must be done. As such, define the sum: $S(t)$ and difference: $D(t)$ of       
  $N_1(t), N_2(t)$ as follows:                                                  
  \begin{align*}                                                                
  S(t) = N_1(t) + N_2(t), \qquad                                                
  D(t) = N_1(t) - N_2(t)                                                        
  \end{align*}                                                                  
  Following the above definition, observe that:                                 
  \begin{align*}                                                                
  \frac{dS}{dt} = \frac{dN_1}{dt} + \frac{dN_2}{dt},                            
  \qquad \frac{dD}{dt} = \frac{dN_1}{dt} - \frac{dN_2}{dt}.                     
  \end{align*}                                                                  
                                                                                
  As mentioned previously, $B$ is a linear function of $N(t)$, thus, we can     
  introduce some constant $k\neq0$ such that $B(N) = kN(t)$, we have:           
  \begin{align*}                                                                
  \frac{dS}{dt}                                                                 
  &= A_1+A_2 - B(N_1)-B(N_2) = A_1 + A_2 - k\bigl[N_1(t)+N_2(t)\bigr] \\        
  &= A_1 + A_2 - kS.                                                            
  \end{align*}                                                                  
  We specify the initial condition $S(0)=S_0$, and solve for $S(t)$ by method   
  of integrating factors. We obtain:                                            
  \begin{align*}                                                                
  S(t) = \frac{A_1+A_2}{k}+ (S_0 - \frac{A_1+A_2}{k})e^{-kt}                    
  \end{align*}                                                                  
  By the linearity of $B(N)$ we can obtain the following expression for         
  $\frac{dD}{dt}$.                                                              
  \begin{align*}                                                                
  \frac{dD}{dt} &= A_1-A_2-B(N_1)+B(N_2)-2\lambda)D(N_1-N_2)\\ &=               
  A_1-A_2-k(N_1-N_2)-2\lambda(N_1-N_2)\\ &= A_1-A_2-(k+2\lambda)D               
  \end{align*}                                                                  
  We specify the initial condition $D(0)=D_0$, by method of integrating         
  factors, we solve for D(t):                                                   
  \begin{align*}                                                                
  D(t) = \frac{A_1-A_2}{k+ 2\lambda} + (D_0 - \frac{A_1-A_2}{k+                 
  2\lambda})e^{-(k+2\lambda)t}                                                  
  \end{align*}                                                                  
  By definition of $S(t)$ and $D(t)$ we have that:                              
  \begin{align*}                                                                
  N_1(t) =  \frac{1}{2}[S(t)+D(t)],\qquad N_2(t) = \frac{1}{2}[S(t)-D(t)]       
  \end{align*}                                                                  
  Thus, by plugging our functions for $S(t)$ and $D(t)$, we find the following  
  closed form solutions for $N_1(t)$ and $N_2(t)$:                              
  \begin{align*}                                                                
  N_1(t)&= \frac{1}{2}[\frac{A_1+A_2}{k}+ (S_0 - \frac{A_1+A_2}{k})e^{-kt} +    
  \frac{A_1-A_2}{k+ 2\lambda} + (D_0 - \frac{A_1-A_2}{k+                        
  2\lambda})e^{-(k+2\lambda)t}]\\                                               
  N_2(t)&= \frac{1}{2}[\frac{A_1+A_2}{k}+ (S_0 - \frac{A_1+A_2}{k})e^{-kt} -    
  \frac{A_1-A_2}{k+ 2\lambda} + (D_0 - \frac{A_1-A_2}{k+                        
  2\lambda})e^{-(k+2\lambda)t}]                                                 
  \end{align*}

⏺ No Caltrans data is in the repo yet. Here's what to pull and what each model
  quantity maps to.

  1. The one dataset that fits: PeMS "Station 5-Minute" (lane-level)

  Go to pems.dot.ca.gov → Data Clearinghouse → Type = Station 5-Minute, District
  (e.g. D4 Bay Area or D7 LA), pick one day. That file is the only common PeMS
  product with per-lane columns, which model 4 requires (N_1, N_2 are two lanes
  of one mainline detector, or two parallel roads).

  Per lane n it gives: Flow_n (veh/5 min), Occupancy_n (fraction), Speed_n (mph,
  often imputed).

  2. Mapping data → model variables

  ┌───────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │     Model     │                          Data                           │
  ├───────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ N_i(t) — cars │ Occupancy_i × (segment length) / (avg vehicle length).  │
  │  in lane i    │ Occupancy is the direct proxy for density; scale it or  │
  │               │ just work in occupancy units.                           │
  ├───────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ A_i — arrival │ Upstream detector's Flow_i, or the ramp flow feeding    │
  │  rate         │ that lane.                                              │
  ├───────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │               │ Slope of a linear fit of Flow_i vs Occupancy_i in the   │
  │ k in B(N)=kN  │ free-flow regime only (occupancy ≲ 0.10–0.15). This is  │
  │               │ where the linear B is defensible.                       │
  ├───────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ λ —           │ Not measured. Fit it: it's the only free parameter once │
  │ lane-change   │  k, A_1, A_2 are set. Estimate from the observed decay  │
  │ rate          │ rate of D(t) = N_1 - N_2 toward equilibrium, since D    │
  │               │ relaxes at rate k + 2λ.                                 │
  └───────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  3. Concrete selection criteria for a good station

  1. % Observed = 100 — avoid imputed lanes; imputation smooths out exactly the
  lane imbalance you're studying.
  2. Mainline (ML) station, no on/off-ramp between it and your upstream A_i
  source — otherwise mass isn't conserved and S(t) won't match.
  3. At least 3 lanes ideally, then use lanes 1 and 2 (leftmost two) — they show
  the strongest imbalance.
  4. Pick a window where traffic is relaxing toward equilibrium, e.g. 5–7am or
  post-peak 7–9pm. Model 4 is linear with a stable fixed point; it cannot
  reproduce congested-regime backward-bending flow.

  4. The validation your closed form makes easy

  D(t) should decay as a single exponential at rate k + 2λ. Plot log|D(t) - D_∞|
  vs t; if it is straight, the model holds and the slope gives you λ directly
  with k already known from step 2. That single plot is the strongest empirical
  result you can get out of this model.

  Pick one file

  Grab a Tuesday, Wednesday, or Thursday — mid-week days have the cleanest, most
  repeatable commute pattern. Avoid Mondays, Fridays, and anything near a
  holiday. From that January list, 2026_01_06, 01_07, or 01_08 (Tue/Wed/Thu) are
  good candidates.

  One file is ~28 MB gzipped, roughly 300–400 MB uncompressed — about 2 million
  rows covering every detector in the district for one day. That's fine; you'll
  filter down to a single station immediately.

  Two things the page doesn't tell you

  1. No header row. The file is bare CSV. The "Field Specification" panel you're
  looking at is the schema — you supply the column names yourself.
  2. Column layout is positional and fixed:

  0  Timestamp        (MM/DD/YYYY HH:MM:SS)
  1  Station ID
  2  District
  3  Freeway #
  4  Direction
  5  Lane Type        (ML, HV, OR, FR, ...)
  6  Station Length
  7  Samples
  8  % Observed
  9  Total Flow
  10 Avg Occupancy
  11 Avg Speed
  then 5 columns per lane, repeating:
     Lane N Samples, Lane N Flow, Lane N Avg Occ, Lane N Avg Speed, Lane N
  Observed

  So lane 1 is columns 12–16, lane 2 is 17–21, lane 3 is 22–26. Columns 13 and 
  18 (Lane 1 Flow, Lane 2 Flow) and columns 14 and 19 (Lane 1 Avg Occ, Lane 2 
  Avg Occ) are the four numbers your entire simulation rests on. Rows have
  trailing empty columns padded out to the max lane count.

  You also need the station metadata file

  The 5-minute file gives you Station ID but not where it is or how many lanes
  it has. Change Type to Station Metadata (same district), and download the
  d04_text_meta_*.txt for a date near your chosen day. That's a small
  tab-delimited file with ID, Fwy, Dir, District, County, City, Abs_PM, 
  Latitude, Longitude, Length, Type, Lanes, Name. You'll use it to find an ML
  station with Lanes >= 3 on a freeway you can reason about (I-80, I-880,
  US-101).

  Order of operations

  1. Download both: one 5-min day file and the matching metadata file.
  2. From metadata, shortlist Type == 'ML' and Lanes >= 3 stations.
  3. From the 5-min file, keep only those station IDs, then rank by mean % 
  Observed (col 8) across the day — you want a station at or near 100.
  4. Pick the top station, extract its 288 rows, and you have N_1(t), N_2(t) as
  occupancy time series.

⏺ Top issue first: model 4's prose and its derivation disagree about what B is.

  1. The B contradiction (fix before simulating)

  Line 205 says the two-lane model carries "the congestion dynamics of the
  congestion model." In paper.tex:102-139 that model is Greenshields, B(N) = v_f
  (N/L)(1 - N/(L ρ_m)) — nonlinear. But line 229 then introduces B(N) = kN and
  the entire S/D decoupling depends on that linearity. With Greenshields, B(N_1)
  + B(N_2) is not a function of S alone (the quadratic term gives N_1² + N_2² =
  (S² + D²)/2), so S and D stay coupled and there is no closed form.

  You have two honest options:

  (a) Keep the closed form, retitle the model — it's "two-lane with linear
  outflow plus lane changing," i.e. it extends \subsection{Linear outflow}, not
  the congestion model. Cleanest, and the closed form survives.

  (b) Keep Greenshields, drop the closed form — simulate numerically (you
  already have the machinery in sim_5.py) and linearize about equilibrium for
  the analysis.

  I recommend (a) for the paper's model 4, and mention (b) as the bridge,
  because of the next point.

  2. The bridge: linear B is Greenshields linearized

  This makes (a) defensible rather than a cop-out. For any smooth B, linearize
  about the symmetric equilibrium N*:

  d(δS)/dt = -B'(N*) δS
  d(δD)/dt = -(B'(N*) + 2λ) δD

  The same two eigenvalues appear, with k replaced by the local slope B'(N*).
  For Greenshields, B'(N) = (v_f/L)(1 - 2N/(L ρ_m)), which is ≈ v_f/L at low
  density and hits zero at capacity. So B(N) = kN is exactly the free-flow limit
  of the congestion model — which is why I said to fit k only where occupancy ≲
  0.15. Above capacity B' < 0 and the sum mode goes unstable, which is a real
  prediction, not a defect.

  3. λ is invisible to S(t) — and that's your best sanity check

  Lane changing conserves total vehicles, so dS/dt = A_1 + A_2 - kS contains no
  λ at all. Structurally:

  - Sum mode: rate k, unaffected by lane changing.
  - Difference mode: rate k + 2λ, always faster since λ > 0.

  So the model's single sharpest empirical claim is: lane imbalance relaxes 
  strictly faster than total occupancy does. If the PeMS data shows D(t)
  decaying slower than S(t), model 4 is falsified outright. That's a genuinely
  testable statement and worth stating explicitly in the paper.

  4. Identifiability — all four parameters are recoverable

  A_1, A_2, k, λ come out of two exponential fits plus two equilibria:

  ┌────────────┬────────────────────┐
  │  Quantity  │        From        │
  ├────────────┼────────────────────┤
  │ k          │ decay rate of S(t) │
  ├────────────┼────────────────────┤
  │ k + 2λ → λ │ decay rate of D(t) │
  ├────────────┼────────────────────┤
  │ A_1 + A_2  │ k · S_∞            │
  ├────────────┼────────────────────┤
  │ A_1 - A_2  │ (k + 2λ) · D_∞     │
  └────────────┴────────────────────┘

  The catch: λ never appears alone, only as k + 2λ. Its standard error is
  inflated by whatever error you have in k, and if the two lanes are nearly
  balanced, D(t) is small and mostly noise. Practical consequence: pick a
  station and time window with a visible, decaying lane imbalance, and report a
  confidence interval on λ, not a point estimate.

  5. Two typos to fix while you're here

  - Line 237: A_1-A_2-B(N_1)+B(N_2)-2\lambda)D(N_1-N_2) — stray ) and a
  duplicated factor. Should read A_1-A_2-B(N_1)+B(N_2)-2\lambda(N_1-N_2).
  - Line 429 (model 6): (D_{1,0}+\frac{A_{1a}-A_{1b}}{...}) has a sign error —
  it must be a minus, as in your line 241. It recurs at lines 434, 438,
  442, 446. Separately, model 6 treats g_1(t) as constant while integrating and
  leaves it inside the equilibrium term; since g is piecewise-constant that's
  only valid within one phase, with matching at the switch times. Want me to
  work through model 6 properly after this?

  Load the d04_text_meta_*.txt (tab-delimited, has a header) and apply:

  2. Lanes == 2 — this is the column that answers your question directly.
  3. Group by (Fwy, Dir) and sort by Abs_PM (absolute postmile).

  2. The ramp check

  For each candidate ML station at postmile p, find the nearest ML station
  upstream of it at p_up. Then require:

  ▎ no station of type OR, FR, or FF has Abs_PM strictly between p_up and p, on 
  ▎ the same Fwy and Dir.

  If that holds, every car passing the upstream detector must pass the
  downstream one, and A_1, A_2 are just the upstream lane flows. This is exactly
  the conservation assumption model 4 needs.

  Direction detail: California postmiles increase heading north and east. So for
  Dir == 'N' or 'E', upstream means smaller Abs_PM; for 'S' and 'W', upstream
  means larger. Getting this backwards silently gives you a segment with ramps
  in it.

⏺ What it does

  Turns point measurements into segment state. PeMS measures flow and speed at a
  detector; model 4's state N_i(t) is the number of cars in a segment, per 
  lane. Without that conversion there's nothing to fit.

  Four steps:

  1. Filter and cache. Read the 150 MB file once, keep the 864 rows for the
  three WB stations, write a small CSV. Every later run loads in milliseconds
  instead of ~20 seconds.
  2. Drop the dead block. Remove rows where both lanes read exactly zero
  occupancy (~08:15–10:00). Left in, they look like an empty road and will drag
  any fit toward it.
  3. Convert flow + speed → vehicle counts. Per lane, using q = ρv:
  ρ_i = flow_i × 12 / speed_i (veh/mi), then N_i = ρ_i × L.
  This is the same conversion the paper used for simulations 1 and 2, so model 4
  stays consistent with them.
  4. Split the roles. N_1, N_2 from downstream 421258; A_1, A_2 from upstream
  421260's lane flows.

  The one judgment call

  I'll use flow ÷ speed rather than occupancy for density, to match the paper.
  Be aware PeMS lists speed at these stations as Estimated, not measured — it's
  back-computed from flow and occupancy via an assumed g-factor. So q/v partly
  unwinds PeMS's own estimate. Occupancy is the more direct measurement but
  needs an assumed effective vehicle length. Neither is clean; matching the
  paper is the better tiebreaker.

  Units

  Time step = 1 = 5 minutes, per the paper's convention. So A is veh/5-min and k
  is per-5-min — which is why k = 0.844 there rather than something in per-hour
  units.

  Output

  A tidy frame indexed by timestamp: N_1, N_2, A_1, A_2, ready for both the

  What the fit will do

  You have 37 time points where all four quantities are measured. The model has
  two unknown numbers:

  - k — how quickly cars drain off the bridge.
  - λ — how quickly drivers switch from the fuller lane to the emptier one.

  Fitting means: search for the pair (k, λ) that makes the model's predicted car
  counts land as close as possible to the measured ones.

  Mechanically, for each candidate pair:

  1. Start the model at the measured car counts at 05:00.
  2. Run it forward through the morning, feeding in the measured arrivals
  A_1(t), A_2(t).
  3. Compare predicted N_1, N_2 against measured N_1, N_2 at all 37 points.
  4. Add up the squared differences — one number saying how wrong that pair is.

  Then pick the pair with the smallest total error.

  What comes out

  1. Numbers: k and λ, with error bars saying how well-determined each is.
  2. A picture: predicted vs measured curves, so you can see where the model
  tracks and where it breaks.
  3. The actual test: refit with λ forced to 0 — no lane changing at all. If the
  fit barely worsens, the data doesn't support the lane-changing term and model
  4 adds nothing over two independent lanes. If it worsens a lot, λ is doing
  real work.

⏺ What Observed means

  PeMS detectors fail constantly — stuck sensors, comms dropouts, hanging
  on/off. Rather than emit gaps, PeMS runs diagnostics on the 30-second samples,
  discards data it judges bad, and imputes replacements from neighbouring
  detectors using regressions fit on historical periods when both were healthy
  (the Chen 2002 method). The 5-minute flow/occupancy/speed fields are therefore
  always populated.

  Observed is how you tell which numbers were real:

  - Lane N Observed — per lane, per 5-minute row. 1 = measured, 0 = imputed.
  - % Observed — the station-level share of underlying samples that were real.

  That's why your screenshot showed 66.7% at EB station 421237: 2 of 3 lanes
  measured, lane 3 imputed. At your westbound stations both lanes read 1 all
  day, so 100%.

  What it does not mean

  Here's the part that matters for your fit. During the 08:05–09:50 outage:

  ts                   five_min  raw_sum  pct_obs  l1_obs
  2026-01-06 08:05:00       0.0      0.0      100       1
  2026-01-06 08:10:00       0.0      0.0      100       1

  Flow is zero, and PeMS reports 100% observed, lane observed = 1. So Observed
  is not a trustworthiness score. It answers only "did PeMS substitute this
  value?" — and a detector reporting a steady stream of zeros hasn't been
  substituted, so it passes.

  You still have to check for physically implausible data yourself, which is
  what the zero-flow filter in load_pems.py does.

  Why raw has no Observed field

  Because raw is pre-imputation — nothing has been replaced, so the flag would
  be uniformly 1. I verified this rather than assuming: 5-minute flow equals the
  sum of the ten underlying 30-second raw flows in 287 of 288 bins (99.65%) for
  your downstream station. For these stations the 5-minute product is a plain
  rollup with no imputation occurring at all.

  The one genuine loss in raw is Samples, the count of good sub-samples behind
  each number. That's the field that separates "no cars passed" from "detector
  dead" — both appear as flow = 0.

  Consequence for your work

  Since the 5-minute data at these stations is unimputed, the two products carry
  identical information; raw just gives it at 10× resolution with measured
  rather than g-factor speeds. Nothing in your earlier 5-minute results is
  contaminated by imputation.

  Why, and it's structural

  A ramp meter releases one vehicle per green, cycling every ~4–15 seconds. Your
  finest available sample is 30 seconds. The gate cycles two to seven times
  within a single sample, so the aggregation destroys exactly the signal g(t)
  represents. This isn't bad luck with these three ramps; no PeMS product can
  resolve a ramp meter.

  Same shape as the λ problem: the phenomenon is faster than the instrument.

❯ would looking at data from other days help with this issue or no?             

⏺ No. It's a resolution limit, not a sample-size limit.

  The meter cycles every 4–15 seconds; your finest sample is 30 seconds. Adding
  days gives you more 30-second samples, but every one of them still averages
  over several complete gate cycles. No amount of additional data recovers
  structure that was destroyed before it was recorded.

  The one theoretical escape would be if the meter ran on a fixed period locked
  to the wall clock — then stacking many days could reconstruct the cycle by
  aliasing. That doesn't apply: Caltrans runs local-responsive metering, where
  the rate is reset continuously from upstream mainline occupancy. There's no
  stable phase to stack against.

  Where more days would help: k in model 4. It's already stable at 2.2–2.4
  across two days, and a third or fourth would tighten the estimate and
  strengthen the replication claim. That's a real, cheap improvement — but it's
  a different problem from the intersection one.

  λ is also resolution-limited, so more days won't fix it either.
